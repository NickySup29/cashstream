What this service covers

Scrutiny and reassessment, handled end to end

When a filed return goes beyond routine automated processing into detailed examination, that's a scrutiny assessment. Where the department believes income has escaped assessment in an earlier year, it can reopen that year through a reassessment notice. Both processes have real deadlines, real documentation requirements, and a real cost to getting the response wrong.

We represent clients through the full lifecycle of both: reviewing the notice, reconstructing the financial position, drafting the submission, representing the case through faceless proceedings or before the jurisdictional officer, and reviewing the final order before advising on next steps.

Receiving a scrutiny notice doesn't mean the department thinks you did something wrong. It means specific figures need verifying. How you respond decides which of those two things it turns into.
Income Tax Act, 2025 update

Which Act actually applies to your notice

If your notice was issued in 2026 or later, it's natural to assume it falls under the new Income Tax Act, 2025. For almost every current scrutiny or reassessment case, that assumption is wrong, and it's worth being precise about why.

Section 536 of the Income Tax Act, 2025, the repeal-and-savings clause, keeps the old Income-tax Act, 1961 governing any proceeding relating to a tax year that began before 1 April 2026. Since scrutiny and reassessment are, by definition, about returns already filed for past years, this covers essentially every live case right now. Your notice almost certainly cites, and should be responded to under, the old Act's section numbers, not the new ones.

The question everyone asks first

What notice did you actually get?

The three most common notices get confused with each other constantly. Here's what each one actually means.

Section 142(1)

“We need more information first.”

Usually issued when a return hasn't been filed, or when the department wants specific accounts, books, or explanations before deciding whether to proceed further. Often a precursor step, not yet a full scrutiny.

Section 143(2)

“Your return has been selected for scrutiny.”

The formal start of a detailed examination. Must be issued within a set window after you file, and leads to a scrutiny assessment order under Section 143(3), or, if you don't engage, a best judgment order under Section 144.

Section 148

“We're reopening an earlier year.”

A reassessment notice, issued when the department believes income from a prior year escaped assessment. Reopening reaches back further than most people expect, and the facts need to be reconstructed, not just recalled.

Common pitfalls

Mistakes that create risk

Ignoring Notices or Missing Deadlines

This is what actually produces an ex-parte best judgment assessment, not the scrutiny itself.

Sending Generic, Unreconciled Replies

A response that doesn't reconcile against AIS/26AS reads as unresponsive even when it isn't meant to.

Not Keeping Records Long Enough

Reassessment can reach back several years, well past when most people stop keeping paperwork.

Treating Scrutiny as Routine

Engaging a professional early changes the outcome more than almost anything done later in the process.

Inconsistent Explanations Across Notices

Inconsistency reads as evasion even when it's just a memory gap.

What's at stake

Getting this wrong compounds quickly, not slowly

  • Additions to income, higher tax demand, and interest exposure, plus penalty exposure for under-reporting or misreporting
  • A best judgment assessment based on the officer's estimate rather than your actual facts
  • Demand recovery proceedings, and bank account attachment in serious cases
  • Escalation into appeal and litigation that a timely, well-documented response could have avoided
  • Reopening of subsequent years once one year has been flagged

Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Why was my return picked up for scrutiny?+
Selection happens either through the department's automated risk-based system (CASS) or manual selection, often triggered by mismatches with AIS or 26AS, large-value transactions, or parameters the department doesn't disclose in advance. Selection alone doesn't imply wrongdoing.
Do I need to appear in person, or can everything be handled online?+
Most scrutiny today runs through faceless assessment, meaning written submissions and, where permitted, video conferencing, with no in-person appearance required in the majority of cases.
How far back can the department reopen my case?+
Reassessment timelines depend on the amount of income believed to have escaped assessment, and can reach back significantly further than most people assume for larger amounts. This is exactly why records need to be kept longer than feels necessary at the time.
What happens if I don't respond in time?+
The case can proceed to a best judgment assessment under the department's own estimate rather than your actual facts, which is rarely favorable and is genuinely avoidable with a timely response.
Will this affect my future returns or increase my audit risk?+
Not automatically, but an unresolved or poorly handled scrutiny can make future selection more likely, and if one year gets flagged, related years sometimes follow.
What if I got a notice about an AIS or 26AS mismatch specifically?+
This is one of the most common scrutiny triggers. It usually means a transaction, deposit, or income entry visible to the department doesn't yet reconcile against your filed return, which is a reconciliation problem to solve, not necessarily an error to admit to before checking.
Do NRIs get scrutiny notices on their Indian income too?+
Yes. Property purchases, high-value remittances, and Indian-sourced income are common triggers for NRIs specifically, and the reconciliation often needs to account for NRE/NRO fund flows that a purely domestic case wouldn't involve.
Can I still respond if I've lost track of records from a few years ago?+
Often, yes, through bank statements, AIS/TIS records, and reconstruction work, but it takes longer and costs more than if the records had been kept. This is worth starting immediately rather than waiting.
What's the difference between scrutiny and reassessment?+
Scrutiny examines a return you've already filed for the same assessment cycle. Reassessment reopens a year that was already assessed, or accepted without scrutiny, because the department believes income escaped taxation in that year.
How much does assessment representation cost?+
Fees depend on the complexity of the notice and the reconstruction work involved, and are discussed transparently during your initial consultation before any work begins.
