When an NRI sells property in India, the buyer must deduct TDS on the full sale price, not just the gain, unless the seller holds a Lower Deduction Certificate. Long-term gains carry 12.5% plus surcharge and cess; short-term gains are taxed at slab rates. Any excess TDS is recovered by filing an income tax return.

Tax rates on immovable property sold by an NRI (before surcharge and 4% cess)

Holding periodSold before 23 July 2024Sold on or after 23 July 2024
More than 24 months (long-term)20% with indexation12.5% without indexation
24 months or less (short-term)Slab ratesSlab rates

The buyer applies these rates to the full sale price unless a Lower Deduction Certificate is in place.

The problem most NRI sellers meet at the last minute

The price is agreed and the buyer is ready. Then the buyer's CA says that about ₹12.5 lakh will be held back from a ₹1 crore sale, even though your actual tax on the gain is closer to ₹7.5 lakh. The difference sits with the Income Tax Department until you file a return and wait for a refund, and the money you planned to move overseas is not there.

This guide explains TDS on sale of property by NRI sellers in India, whether the asset is a flat, house, plot or other immovable property, through the 15 questions we hear most often, in the order the sale happens: before the sale, on sale day and after the sale. Each answer is short enough to read in half a minute, with a link to a deeper guide where the topic needs one.

Illustrative example: the gap between your tax and the TDS

Illustrative figures, simplified, before surcharge and cess. Long-term gain, sold after 23 July 2024.

ItemAmount
Sale price₹1,00,00,000
Cost of acquisition plus improvements₹40,00,000
Capital gain₹60,00,000
Tax on the gain at 12.5%₹7,50,000
TDS without a certificate (12.5% of the sale price)₹12,50,000
Cash held back until the refund₹5,00,000

With a Lower Deduction Certificate, the buyer deducts at a rate based on your computed tax, so the amount held back is close to the tax actually due.

The sale in six steps

  1. 1

    Three to four months before the sale: apply for the Lower Deduction Certificate (Form 128 on TRACES).

  2. 2

    In the same window: finalise the sale agreement.

  3. 3

    Complete the sale paperwork through a professional so the registration date stays close to the date used in the certificate application.

  4. 4

    Execute the sale with an experienced lawyer.

  5. 5

    After the sale: file your income tax return with professional help.

  6. 6

    Claim any refund if the certificate was not applied or the rate was higher. If the certificate was applied, only the reporting compliance remains.

If you are the buyer: what to know

  • •

    Confirm the seller's residential status before any payment, because the deduction rules differ for residents and non-residents.

  • •

    Deduct on the full sale value at the applicable rate, unless the seller gives you a valid Lower Deduction Certificate that names you.

  • •

    From 1 October 2026, an eligible resident individual or HUF buyer can use a PAN instead of a TAN. Other buyers still need a TAN.

  • •

    Deposit the tax and file the TDS statement within the due dates, and give the seller the TDS certificate so they can claim credit.

  • •

    Keep the challan and certificate, since the sub-registrar may ask for proof of payment.

Four mistakes that cost NRI sellers money

  • •

    Applying late or not at all. Buyers' CAs often do not raise the certificate, so sellers who do not know about it bear the higher rate.

  • •

    Missing or late return. Not filing on time can invite scrutiny proceedings for the year of sale, with possible penalties.

  • •

    Incomplete or vague answers to the officer. Missing documents, evasive replies and misleading information are the usual causes of delay.

  • •

    Going without professional or legal help. The certificate, the sale deed and the return must tell the same story.

Key terms used in this guide

It is not difficult, but the order of steps matters. Apply for a Lower Deduction Certificate three to four months before the expected sale, finalise the sale agreement in that window, complete registration with an experienced lawyer, then file your income tax return. Most problems come from starting the certificate late. You do not need to inform any authority in India before selling, unlike some countries where a prior notice is expected. The compliance happens through the buyer's TDS, the certificate and your return afterwards. Read more: NRI tax relocation advisory

Tax depends on how long you held the property and when you sold it. Held over 24 months and sold on or after 23 July 2024, the gain is long-term and taxed at 12.5% without indexation. Held 24 months or less, it is short-term and taxed at slab rates. Surcharge and 4% cess are added. For sales before 23 July 2024 the long-term rate was 20% with indexation. The option to choose between the two rates for older property is open only to resident individuals and HUFs, so an NRI should not expect to use indexation.

Without a certificate, the buyer deducts TDS on the entire sale price, not on your profit. For long-term gains this works out to roughly 13% to 15% once surcharge and cess are included, with the surcharge rising as the sale value crosses ₹50 lakh and ₹1 crore. Short-term gains are deducted at slab-based rates. TDS on sale of property by NRI sellers above ₹1 crore feels heavier because the same 12.5% is applied to the whole price and the surcharge steps up. The buyer's duty to deduct sits in Section 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (it was Section 195 earlier), and unlike a sale by a resident, there is no minimum value below which TDS is skipped for an NRI seller. Yes. An NRI selling property can apply under Section 395(1) of the Income Tax Act, 2025, using Form 128, the successor to Form 13. You do not need to visit India: the application, officer queries and certificate download happen online through the TRACES taxpayer login, and a professional can act for you. Any person, resident or non-resident, can apply under Section 395. Filing is optional and is done only on TRACES: log in, go to Dashboard, then e-file and view, then File Forms, then Form 128. A PAN is mandatory, and on submission you receive an Acknowledgment Receipt Number. The application must include a computation of estimated income and tax for the year. Registration of the sale deed follows the local registrar's rules, so a registered power of attorney may be needed if you cannot attend. Source: Form 128 FAQs, Income Tax Department. Read more: Lower Deduction Certificate for NRI property sale

The certificate is issued for the sale of the property and names the buyer who will deduct the tax. If there are several buyers, each can be named. It applies to the payments and period stated in the certificate, so a change of buyer or a sale slipping into the next tax year can need a fresh application. Name every buyer in the application. A buyer who is not on the certificate cannot rely on it.

Keep the seller's PAN and Aadhaar if held, a draft sale agreement, the cost of acquisition and any improvement costs, a capital gains computation, and the previous year's income tax return. You also need the expected sale date, the stamp duty value fixed by the registrar, and details of any other Indian losses to set off. These are the same papers the Assessing Officer usually asks for. Under Rule 213 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026 the officer looks at the tax on your estimated income, your tax position over the previous four years, any existing liabilities, and the advance tax and TDS credits available, so your computation must match your return history. Having everything ready before filing is the single biggest way to avoid delay.

In our experience, a certificate has taken between 15 and 60 days from filing. Delays usually come from missing documents, incomplete replies to the officer's queries, or vague explanations. Because the range is wide, apply three to four months before the sale and plan the payment schedule around it. If you are three weeks from closing and have not applied, ask the buyer for an extension, request expedited disposal, and engage a professional who handles the relevant jurisdiction so the application goes in at once.

The certificate works for payments made after it is issued, so an advance deducted earlier stays at the full rate. The capital gains tax itself follows the date of the legal transfer, so the excess is recovered later through your return. Where the contract allows, ask the buyer to hold the next payment until the certificate arrives. The Income Tax Department's own FAQ says the certificate must reach the buyer before tax is deducted and cannot be processed once the transaction is complete. If your estimates change, a fresh application can be made. How an earlier advance is treated is a grey area, so have a professional review the payment schedule before the agreement is signed.

A seller without a PAN faces a higher TDS rate, because the buyer must deduct at the higher of the normal rate and 20%. Get a PAN before the sale, since the certificate application, the return and any refund all depend on it. Applying early avoids a cash block that a PAN would have prevented. Form 128 cannot even be submitted without a PAN, so start the PAN application as soon as a sale is planned. Read more: Lower Deduction Certificate page

From 1 October 2026, a resident individual or HUF buying property from an NRI can deposit the TDS using their PAN, without obtaining a TAN. The tax is paid and reported through a PAN-based challan-cum-statement on the Income Tax portal, under CBDT Notification 121/2026. The seller's Lower Deduction Certificate is a separate step. Companies, firms and other buyers are not covered and still need a TAN. Some sub-registrar offices may ask for proof of the TDS payment at registration, so keep the challan and the TDS certificate. Check with your buyer's CA which route is being used, since many buyers will still be familiar with the old TAN and Form 27Q process.

A nil certificate is possible where the sale produces a loss, since no tax arises. In practice, loss cases often receive a nominal rate of 0.1% instead of zero, though zero certificates have been issued. The outcome depends on the officer, the range and the jurisdiction, so it cannot be promised. A certificate is also not always worth pursuing. If you have other liabilities, if the cost documents are not ready and a valuation will take time, and the sale is urgent, the delay can outweigh the saving.

Yes. An NRI must report the sale in an Indian income tax return, usually due by 31 July after the financial year, even if a certificate was used. Late or missed filing can lead to scrutiny proceedings for the year of sale and penalties, and it is also the only route to a refund. Use a professional for the return, because the capital gains computation must match the figures used in the certificate and the buyer's TDS return.

File your income tax return and claim the TDS credit shown in your Form 26AS or AIS. Refunds are not claimed from the buyer. In our experience, refunds have taken between one and seven months, depending on processing and the matching of the TDS credit. Check that the buyer has filed the TDS return and that the credit appears against your PAN before you file.

Yes. If the sale gives a loss, or the tax payable is lower than the TDS deducted, the excess is refundable through your return. Without a certificate, the buyer still deducts on the full sale value, so the refund can be large. A nil or lower certificate avoids locking that cash up in the first place. The refund still depends on filing the return and on the buyer's TDS credit appearing in your records.

A long-term gain on a residential property can be exempt if you reinvest in a residential house in India. If you cannot buy before filing your return, deposit the amount in the Capital Gains Account Scheme before the due date, usually 31 July. Conditions apply, so confirm eligibility before relying on the exemption. This exemption was Section 54 under the earlier Act.
