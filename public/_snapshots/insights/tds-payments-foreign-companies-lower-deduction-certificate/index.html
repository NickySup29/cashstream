When TDS applies to a payment to a foreign company

TDS on foreign payments applies when the payment is chargeable to tax in India in the hands of the foreign company. The payer, whether company, firm or individual, deducts it at the earlier of crediting the amount in the books or paying it. The same rule governs payments to non resident vendors.

Under the Income-tax Act 2025 this sits in Section 393(2), which replaces Section 195 of the old Act. Before the money goes abroad, the payer also files Form 145 (formerly Form 15CA), and usually Form 146 (formerly Form 15CB) from a chartered accountant.

The main trap is that the payer carries the risk. A short or missed deduction makes the payer an assessee in default, with interest of 1% a month until deduction and 1.5% a month until deposit, a possible penalty, and loss of the expense deduction until the tax is paid.

TDS by payment type: the matrix

TDS on a foreign remittance depends on what the payment is for, not who receives it. Start with the Act rate (the sec 195 TDS rates, now under Section 393(2)), compare it with the treaty rate, and apply the lower one only with a Tax Residency Certificate (TRC, issued by the foreign tax authority to show residence) and Form 41 (formerly Form 10F).

Royalty is commonly taxed at 20% under the Act. So are fees for technical services and interest. This is the usual Section 195 rate for each type, now under Section 393(2). Rates below are before 4% cess and a surcharge of 2% or 5% on higher payments. Treaty rates vary by country, so the ranges are typical, not fixed.

Who needs a lower deduction certificate, and who does not

A certificate pays off when the treaty rate is not available or not certain, or when payments are large or recurring and the real tax is far below the gross deduction. Typical cases are services that may not be taxable in India at all, and a capital gain where the payer would otherwise deduct on the full price.

People search for a lower deduction certificate under Section 195. Strictly, a certificate for a foreign payee comes from Section 197 (now Section 395(1)), or from an order on the payer's application under Section 195(2) (now Section 395(2)). A lower deduction certificate is not needed when the treaty rate is clear, the TRC and Form 41 are in hand, and the payer is comfortable relying on them. Then the 40 to 45 days a certificate typically takes add little.

Who applies: the foreign company or the payer

Either side can apply, and the two routes do different things.

The foreign company applies on Form 128 under Section 395(1) (formerly Form 13 under Section 197). This is the lower deduction certificate for non resident companies. It names the payers and sets a lower or nil rate for the year, up to a stated amount.

The Indian payer applies on Form 129 under Section 395(2) (formerly Form 15E under Section 195(2)), asking the officer to decide how much of a specific payment is chargeable. It is filed before the remittance and covers that payment.

When a certificate is not the right tool

Often the payment can be handled without one.

Treaty relief. With a TRC and Form 41, the payer can apply the lower treaty rate directly.

Form 146. A chartered accountant certifies the nature of the payment, the rate and the treaty position before the remittance. This is the normal route for most payments.

No permanent establishment (PE). If the foreign company has no fixed place of business or dependent agent in India, business income such as commission is usually not taxable here. This is the position for a foreign company with no PE in India, supported by a no PE declaration.

Payment not chargeable. If the income is not taxable in India, there is nothing to deduct. Record the reasoning on file.

We ask three things: is the income clearly taxable, is the treaty rate clearly available, and is the payer willing to rely on it? If all three are yes, a certificate is usually not needed.

Papers a foreign payee needs to provide

The foreign specific papers are: a TRC for the relevant year; Form 41 (formerly Form 10F), filed online by the foreign company; a signed no PE declaration; the contract or invoice showing what the payment is for; and its PAN, or, if it has none, the details the Rules allow so that the higher no PAN rate is not applied. For the full documents required for lower deduction certificate cases, read Documents Needed for a Lower Deduction Certificate.

Can a foreign company claim a refund of TDS deducted in India?

Yes. A foreign company claims a TDS refund by filing an Indian income tax return in Form ITR-6, the return for companies. A return is needed when the company has income from India and tax was deducted on it. The refund of excess TDS usually arises because the treaty rate is lower than the rate deducted, or because the income is not taxable in India at all.

The refund claim needs an Indian PAN, a registered authorised signatory and a digital signature. In our files a power of attorney has not been required. The TDS credit must show against the company's PAN in Form 26AS (now Form 168) or AIS. If it does not, read TDS Not in Form 26AS or AIS.

We do not attach Form 16A (now Form 131), the tax residency certificate or Form 41 to the return, but we keep them ready for queries. The refund goes to a validated Indian bank account. In our files such refunds have mostly come in about six months or less, and no timeline can be promised. If the refund is stuck, read NRI TDS Refund Stuck or Failed. To claim a refund of excess TDS for a foreign company, see our TDS refund service.

Do not confuse this with Form 67. Form 67 claims credit in India for tax paid abroad, which is a different question.

Mistakes payers make

These are the four mistakes we see most.

  1. 1

    Deducting at the wrong rate. The domestic rate where a treaty applies, or the treaty rate without a TRC. Too much locks up the vendor's cash. Too little makes the payer an assessee in default.

  2. 2

    Ignoring a pending certificate. Remitting before it arrives means the default rate applies, and the excess comes back only through the vendor's refund claim.

  3. 3

    Wrong payment type. Treating technical fees as business income, or royalty as a service fee, changes the rate and whether tax is due at all.

  4. 4

    Grossing up errors. Where the contract says the payer bears the tax, tax is deducted on the grossed up amount. A mistake here means a short deduction and interest.

For the nil and lower distinction, read Nil vs Lower TDS Certificate.

Before you remit: a quick check

Answer yes or no before paying a foreign vendor.

  1. 1

    Is the payment chargeable to tax in India?

  2. 2

    Have you identified the payment type from the contract?

  3. 3

    Do you have the vendor's TRC for this year?

  4. 4

    Has the vendor filed Form 41?

  5. 5

    Do you have a no PE declaration, if you rely on one?

  6. 6

    Does the vendor have a PAN?

  7. 7

    Is a lower deduction certificate in force for this payment, and is it within its amount and date?

  8. 8

    Have Form 145, and Form 146 where needed, been prepared before the remittance?

A no to any of these is a reason to Book a Consultation.
